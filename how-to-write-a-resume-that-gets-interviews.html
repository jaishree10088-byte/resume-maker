How to Write a Resume That Gets Interviews: A Fresher's Guide

ResumeMaker Blog · 14 November 2026 · 6 min read

Your resume has one job: to get you an interview. It is not your life story, and it is not a form you fill in once and forget. For a fresher in India — applying for your first job after college, a diploma, or a training course — the resume is often the only thing a recruiter sees before deciding whether to call you. This guide explains, step by step, how to write a resume that earns that call.

Start With the Job, Not With Yourself

Most freshers write their resume first and then send the same file to every opening. That is the single biggest reason resumes get ignored. Before you write a word, read the job description carefully and underline the skills, tools and qualities it asks for. If the posting mentions Excel, data entry, customer handling or a specific programming language, those words need to appear on your resume — honestly, and only if they are true for you. A resume that mirrors the job description tells the recruiter, in seconds, that you are relevant. A generic resume tells them nothing.

Keep It to One Page

As a fresher, one page is enough — and it is also what recruiters prefer. A recruiter spends only seconds on a first scan, so every line must earn its place. If your resume runs to two pages with a long list of hobbies and a paragraph about your school days, the important parts get buried. One page forces you to choose: keep what proves you can do the job, cut the rest. Use a clean layout with clear headings — Contact, Summary, Skills, Education, Projects — and leave white space so the page is easy to read.

Write a Short, Specific Summary

At the top of your resume, below your name and contact details, write two or three lines that say who you are and what you offer. Avoid empty phrases like "hardworking and motivated individual seeking a challenging position". Instead, be specific: "B.Com graduate with strong Excel and Tally skills, looking for an accounts assistant role. Completed a three-month internship at a local CA firm, where I prepared GST invoices and maintained ledgers." A good summary answers the recruiter's first question — "what can this person do for us?" — in a single breath.

Show Skills With Proof

Listing skills is easy; proving them is what matters. Do not just write "communication skills" — show where you used them. Your final-year project, an internship, a college event you organised, a part-time job, freelance work, or even a personal project all count as proof. Describe each one in one or two lines with an action and a result: "Built a billing web app for a local shop using HTML and JavaScript; the shop now uses it daily." If you have no work experience at all, your projects and coursework are your experience — give them the same space a job would get.

Use Numbers Where You Can

Numbers make a resume concrete. "Handled customer queries" is vague; "answered 30 to 40 customer calls a day during a two-month internship" is real. You do not need big numbers — small, honest ones work just as well: the size of a team you led, the number of pages you typed, the marks you scored, the number of events you managed. Never invent numbers. If a recruiter checks and finds an exaggeration, your application is finished.

Format for Humans and Machines

Many companies in India now use applicant tracking software, which reads your resume before a human does. Keep the formatting simple so both can read it: standard section headings, one clear font, no text boxes, no tables for layout, no images or icons inside the text. Save the file as a PDF unless the job posting asks for something else. And check the file on your phone — if it looks messy on a small screen, fix it before sending.

Proofread Like It Matters

A spelling mistake in a resume is not a small thing. To a recruiter it signals carelessness, and for a fresher with no track record, care is the only thing you can prove on paper. Read your resume aloud, slowly, and check every word. Then ask a friend or teacher to read it too — a fresh pair of eyes catches what yours skips. Check your phone number and email address twice: a perfect resume with a wrong digit never gets a call.

Writing a good resume is a skill, and like any skill it improves with practice. Build your first version, send it to a few jobs, and watch what happens. If you get no replies, change one thing at a time — the summary, the skills, the order of sections — until the calls start coming. The resume that gets interviews is rarely the first draft. It is the one you kept improving.
