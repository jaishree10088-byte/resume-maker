Interview Preparation Checklist: The Week Before, the Day Before, the Day Of

ResumeMaker Blog · 14 November 2026 · 7 min read

Most interviews are won before the candidate sits down. The people who perform well are rarely the most talented in the room — they are the ones who prepared. This checklist breaks interview preparation into three stages: the week before, the day before, and the day itself. Follow it, and you will walk in calm, ready and confident.

The Week Before: Research and Practice

Start with the company. Read its website, especially the about page and the products or services it offers. Look up recent news about it, and check its social media pages to understand how it talks about itself. Then re-read the job description and match it against your own resume: for every skill it asks for, prepare one example of how you have used it. If it asks for Excel, be ready to talk about a real spreadsheet you built; if it asks for customer handling, have a real story ready.

Next, prepare your answers to the questions almost every interviewer asks. "Tell me about yourself" is not an invitation to tell your life story — it is a request for a one-minute summary of who you are and why you fit this role. Prepare a short version of your education, your most relevant experience or project, and why you want this job. Also prepare answers for your strengths, a weakness you are working on, and why you are leaving your current job or why you chose this field. Practise saying these answers aloud, not just thinking them — speaking is a different skill from thinking, and the first time you say an answer should not be in the interview room.

Learn the STAR Method for Your Stories

When interviewers ask for examples — "Tell me about a time you solved a problem" — they want a story with a shape. Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Describe the situation briefly, explain what you had to do, say what you actually did, and finish with what happened. Prepare three or four such stories from your studies, projects, internships or part-time work. A fresher can use a college project, an event they organised, or a problem they fixed during an internship. One well-told story answers more than a dozen claims about your skills.

Prepare Questions to Ask Them

Almost every interview ends with "Do you have any questions for us?" Saying "no" is a missed chance. Prepare two or three genuine questions: what a typical day in the role looks like, what the team is working on right now, how training works for new joiners, or what success in the first three months would look like. Good questions show that you are serious about the job — and they help you decide whether you actually want it.

The Day Before: Logistics and Rest

The day before, take care of everything that can go wrong. Print two or three copies of your resume on clean paper and keep them in a folder, along with your certificates, a photo ID and a pen. If the interview is in person, plan your route and check the travel time, then add extra time for traffic — in Indian cities, this matters more than candidates expect. Choose your clothes the night before: clean, ironed, and appropriate for the workplace. Eat well and sleep on time. A tired candidate forgets answers they knew perfectly the day before.

The Day Of: Arrive Ready

Reach the venue ten to fifteen minutes early. Switch your phone to silent before you enter. Greet the interviewer with a smile and a clear introduction. Listen to each question fully before answering, and if you do not understand a question, ask for it to be repeated — that is far better than answering the wrong question. Speak clearly and at a natural pace. If you do not know an answer, say so honestly and explain how you would find out, rather than guessing. Be polite to everyone you meet in the office, from the receptionist onwards — interviewers often ask staff what they thought of a candidate.

After the Interview

When you get home, send a short thank-you message or email the same day, mentioning the role and thanking the interviewer for their time. Keep it brief — two or three lines is enough. Then wait. If the interviewer gave you a timeline, respect it; if a week passes with no news, one polite follow-up message is reasonable. Whatever the result, write down the questions you were asked while you remember them. They are the best preparation for your next interview.
